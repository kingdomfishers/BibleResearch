# The Bronze Serpent Trajectory: Textual Evidence, Wordplay, Intermediate Bridges, and Contrasting Motifs in Numbers 21:4–9, 2 Kings 18:4, and John 3:14–15

## 1. Epistemic Framework and Methodological Distinctions

This study examines the intertextual and theological trajectory connecting three primary biblical passages—**Numbers 21:4–9**, **2 Kings 18:4**, and **John 3:14–15**—along with the canonical and Second Temple bridges that mediate between them. To maintain analytical precision, findings are organized across five distinct epistemic categories:

1. **Attested Textual and Original-Language Evidence:** Observed lexical forms, morphology, syntax, and textual features in the Masoretic Text (MT), Septuagint (LXX), and Greek New Testament (GNT), as well as extant Second Temple and early Jewish sources.
2. **Linguistic and Grammatical Relationships:** Etymologies, paronomasia (wordplay), voice/aspect shifts, and semantic transitions across Hebrew and Greek witnesses.
3. **Structural and Rhetorical Parallels:** Shared narrative sequences, typological framing formulas, and syntactic correspondences.
4. **Physical and Material Analogies:** Sensory, metallurgical, military, and spatial features (e.g., copper/bronze coloration, desert snakes, elevated signal-poles, crucifixion).
5. **Interpretive Hypotheses:** Theological syntheses, historical reconstructions, and intertextual inferences proposed to explain *why* these connections operate as they do.

## 2. Numbers 21:4–9: Crisis, Wordplay, and the Remedial Gaze

### 2.1 Attested Textual Evidence and Narrative Arc

Numbers 21:4–9 sits at a pivotal transition in the wilderness itinerary as Israel journeys from Mount Hor toward the Reed Sea (`yam sûp`) to bypass Edom (v. 4). The narrative unfolds in five concise movements:

- **Impatience and Complaint (vv. 4–5):** The people's spirit/life-breath grows "short" (`wattiqṣar nepeš-hāʿām baddārek`), and they speak against God and Moses, complaining of the lack of bread and water and expressing loathing for "this worthless/light bread" (`halleḥem haqqəlōqēl`, from the root `qll`, "light, trifling, contemptible").
- **Judgment via Fiery Serpents (v. 6):** YHWH sends against the people `hannəḥāšîm haśśərāpîm` ("the serpents, the fiery/burning ones"), which bite (`waynaššəkû`) the people so that many die. The LXX translates the phrase as `τοὺς ὄφεις τοὺς θανατοῦντας` ("the deadly serpents"), shifting focus from the burning quality to lethality.
- **Confession and Intercession (v. 7):** The people acknowledge sin (`ḥāṭāʾnû`) in speaking against YHWH and Moses, asking Moses to pray (`hitpallēl`) that YHWH remove (`wəyāsēr`) "the serpent" (`hannāḥāš`, collective singular).
- **Divine Remedy (v. 8):** YHWH instructs Moses: `ʿăśēh ləkā śārāp wəśîm ʾōtô ʿal-nēs wəhāyāh kol-hannāšûk wərāʾāh ʾōtô wāḥāy` ("Make for yourself a *śārāp* and set it on a *nēs*, and everyone who is bitten and sees [`rāʾāh`] it shall live [`wāḥāy`]").
- **Execution and Healing (v. 9):** Moses crafts `nəḥaš nəḥōšet` ("a serpent of bronze/copper") and sets it on the `nēs`. Whenever a serpent bites a person, "and he gazes intently toward [`wəhibbîṭ ʾel`] the bronze serpent, he lives [`wāḥāy`]."

### 2.2 Linguistic Relationships and Wordplay

Numbers 21:4–9 exhibits dense Hebrew paronomasia and subtle lexical shifts between YHWH's command (v. 8) and Moses' execution (v. 9):

1. **`śārāp` (שָׂרָף) vs. `nāḥāš` (נָחָשׁ) and `nəḥōšet` (נְחֹשֶׁת):**
   - In v. 8, YHWH commands Moses to make a `śārāp` (related to the verb `śārap`, "to burn"), without specifying the material. In v. 9, Moses makes a `nəḥaš nəḥōšet` ("a serpent of copper/bronze").
   - The phrase `nəḥaš nəḥōšet` creates unmistakable assonance and consonantal wordplay on the shared triad **`n-ḥ-š`** (`נחשׁ`). In Biblical Hebrew, `nāḥāš` (serpent) and `nəḥōšet` (copper/bronze) sound virtually paired, and the root `nḥš` also carries a homonymous sense of "divination/enchantment" (cf. Gen 44:5; Num 23:23; 24:1; 2 Kgs 17:17; 21:6)—a latent lexical resonance that anticipates later anxieties about magical or idolatrous misuse.
2. **The Shift in Verbs of Looking (`rāʾāh` -> `hibbîṭ`):**
   - YHWH's promise (v. 8) uses the ordinary verb of perception, `rāʾāh` (Qal, "to see").
   - The narrative fulfillment (v. 9) shifts to `hibbîṭ` (Hiphil of `nbṭ`, "to look intently, gaze steadfastly toward [`ʾel`]"), a verb frequently associated in the Hebrew Bible with attentive, expectant, or trusting regard (e.g., Ps 34:6 [Eng 34:5]; Isa 51:1–2; Zech 12:10).
   - The LXX similarly distinguishes the verbs or intensifies the gaze (`ἐπιβλέπω` in v. 9, "to look upon/regard attentively"), reinforcing that the act is not a casual glance but an intentional orientation toward the divinely appointed sign.
3. **The Standard / Pole (`nēs`, נֵס):**
   - `nēs` does not mean merely a bare stake; across the Hebrew Bible it denotes a raised military ensign, signal-pole, or rallying banner (e.g., Exod 17:15, `YHWH nissî`; Isa 5:26; 11:10, 12; 13:2; 18:3; 49:22; 62:10; Jer 4:6; 51:12).
   - Crucially, the LXX translates `nēs` in Num 21:8–9 with **`σημεῖον`** (*sēmeion*, "sign, signal, standard"), establishing a major Greek lexical bridge to Wisdom 16:6 and Johannine "sign" theology.

### 2.3 Physical Analogies and Interpretive Hypotheses

- **Physical/Material Analogy:** Burnished copper or bronze (`nəḥōšet`) naturally glints reddish-gold in desert sunlight, visually evoking "fire" (`śārāp`) and bridging YHWH's command (`śārāp`) with Moses' metallic craft (`nəḥaš nəḥōšet`). Likewise, the burning inflammation of venomous desert snakebite (cf. the saw-scaled viper or horned viper of the Arabah/Sinai) gives sensory grounding to "fiery serpents."
- **Interpretive Hypotheses:**
  - *Irony of the Reversed Instrument:* Rather than removing the snakes as the people requested (`wəyāsēr`, v. 7), YHWH leaves the threat in place and elevates the image of the very agent of judgment as the medium of deliverance. Healing requires confronting the emblem of their sin and mortality.
  - *Non-Autonomous Efficacy:* Textually, the narrative attributes no independent potency to the bronze object; healing follows obedience to YHWH's spoken directive (`wayyōʾmer YHWH`, v. 8).

## 3. 2 Kings 18:4: Cultic Degeneration and the Crushing of *Nehushtan*

### 3.1 Attested Textual Evidence

Within the Deuteronomistic evaluation of King Hezekiah's Judean reforms (2 Kgs 18:3–6), v. 4 enumerates four decisive iconoclastic acts:

> "He removed the high places (`bāmôt`), and shattered the pillars (`maṣṣēbôt`), and cut down the Asherah (`ʾăšērāh`), and crushed in pieces (`kittat`) the bronze serpent (`nəḥaš hannəḥōšet`) that Moses had made (`ʾăšer-ʿāśāh mōšeh`), for until those days the Israelites were burning incense/making smoke-offerings (`məqaṭṭərîm`) to it (`lô`); and he [or: one] called it *Nəḥuštān* (`wayyiqrāʾ-lô nəḥuštān`)."

### 3.2 Linguistic Analysis and Wordplay on *Nəḥuštān* (נְחֻשְׁתָּן)

1. **Morphology and Double Resonance:** The name `nəḥuštān` builds on `nəḥōšet` (copper/bronze) with the `-ān` suffix (expressing either material essence, a diminutive/contemptuous nuance, or an adjectival/appellative form). Because `nəḥōšet` shares the `n-ḥ-š` consonantal frame with `nāḥāš` (serpent), the name fuses "serpent" and "bronze" into a single label.
2. **Grammatical Ambiguity of `wayyiqrāʾ-lô`:** In Biblical Hebrew, a third-person masculine singular verb without an explicit new subject can refer back to Hezekiah ("and *he* called it Nehushtan") or function impersonally ("and *it was called* Nehushtan").
   - *Interpretive Hypothesis A (Hezekiah's polemical naming):* Hezekiah disparagingly reduces a venerated relic to "a mere piece of bronze" / "little bronze-snake thing," stripping it of sacral aura before/while pulverizing it.
   - *Interpretive Hypothesis B (Pre-existing cultic epithet):* Israelites had given the artifact a proper cultic name (*Nehushtan*), treating it as a minor deity or apotropaic fetish, which the Deuteronomistic narrator records to explain its idolatrous status.
3. **The Verb `kittat` (כִּתַּת, Piel of `ktt`):** Meaning "to beat fine, hammer/crush to pieces," `kittat` echoes the vocabulary of idol destruction elsewhere in the Deuteronomistic and Pentateuchal traditions (e.g., Deut 9:21, where Moses crushes/grinds the golden calf; cf. Isa 30:14; Mic 1:7). The parallel is striking: Moses made the bronze serpent in Num 21:9, yet Hezekiah treats its corrupted survival with the same radical destruction Moses applied to the golden calf.

### 3.3 Contrasting Motifs: Numbers 21:4–9 vs. 2 Kings 18:4

| Motif | Numbers 21:4–9 | 2 Kings 18:4 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Origin & Legitimacy** | Made by Moses (`wayyaʿaś mōšeh`) at YHWH's explicit command. | Historically traced to Moses (`ʾăšer-ʿāśāh mōšeh`), yet destroyed by royal reform. |
| **Action on the Object** | Elevated on a standard (`nēs`) to be seen. | Crushed to pieces (`kittat`) alongside `bāmôt`, `maṣṣēbôt`, and `ʾăšērāh`. |
| **Human Posture** | Obedient looking (`hibbîṭ ʾel`) in crisis, mediated by YHWH's promise. | Cultic veneration (`məqaṭṭərîm lô`, offering incense *to it*) across generations. |
| **Function of the Sign** | Pointer beyond itself to YHWH's saving word. | Terminus of worship; a relic-turned-idol threatening exclusive Yahwism. |
| **Physical/Historical Analogy** | Comparative ANE archaeology attests copper/bronze serpent figurines in Levantine sanctuaries (e.g., Timna, Hazor, Gezer, Megiddo) linked to metallurgy, healing, or apotropaic rites. *(Note: The biblical text itself neither locates Num 21 at Timna nor endorses apotropaic magic; this remains an external comparative analogy.)* | Hezekiah's purge aligns with covenantal aniconism (Exod 20:4–5; Deut 4:15–19): even a divinely instituted sign loses legitimacy when sacralized as an independent power. |

## 4. Intermediate Canonical, Second Temple, and Early Jewish Bridges

The transition from a *destroyed idolatrous relic* in 2 Kings 18:4 to a *positive christological type* in John 3:14–15 does not occur in a vacuum. Several textual and theological bridges explain how the bronze serpent was rehabilitated as a non-idolatrous "sign" pointing solely to God's word and saving action.

### 4.1 Canonical Old Testament Bridges

1. **Deuteronomy 8:15–16:** Recounts the wilderness as a place of "fiery serpents [`nāḥāš śārāp`] and scorpions," framing the desert terrors within YHWH's testing (`lənassōtəkā`) and sustaining providence.
2. **Isaiah 6:1–6:** Combines two key motifs from Numbers 21: YHWH's throne is "high and lifted up" (`rām wəniśśāʾ`, v. 1), and the attendants are `śərāpîm` ("seraphim," same root `śrp`, vv. 2, 6), one of whom purges Isaiah's sin with a live coal—associating "fiery ones" and "lifted-up" imagery with atonement and divine holiness rather than demonic magic.
3. **Isaiah 45:22:** "Turn/look to me and be saved [`pənû-ʾēlay wəhiwwāšəʿû`], all the ends of the earth!" (LXX: `ἐπιστράφητε πρός με καὶ σωθήσεσθε`). This universalizes the salvific gaze directly to YHWH, detaching healing/salvation from any physical image.
4. **Isaiah 52:13 (Crucial LXX Lexical Bridge):** The fourth Servant Song opens: `hinnēh yaśkîl ʿabdî yārûm wəniśśāʾ wəgābah məʾōd` ("Behold, my servant shall act wisely; he shall be high and lifted up and greatly exalted"). The LXX translates `yārûm wəniśśāʾ` using **`ὑψωθήσεται`** (`ὑψόω`, "he shall be lifted up/exalted") and `δοξασθήσεται` ("glorified"). This provides the exact Greek verb (`ὑψόω`) that John 3:14, 8:28, and 12:32–34 use to fuse physical elevation (crucifixion) with divine exaltation.
5. **Zechariah 12:10:** "They shall look upon [`wəhibbîṭû`, Hiphil of `nbṭ`] me/him whom they have pierced [`dāqārû`]." Zechariah employs the exact Hebrew verb of gazing (`hibbîṭ`) found in Num 21:9, and John 19:37 explicitly cites Zech 12:10 (`ὄψονται εἰς ὃν ἐξεκέντησαν`) at the foot of the cross—the narrative climax of Jesus being "lifted up."

### 4.2 Deuterocanonical and Hellenistic Jewish Bridges

1. **Wisdom of Solomon 16:5–12 (Primary Theological Bridge):**
   - Writing in Greek, Wisdom 16 explicitly reinterprets Numbers 21:4–9 within an anti-idolatrous framework (cf. Wis 13–15):
     - **v. 6:** When bitten by crooked serpents, Israel was troubled briefly for admonition, "having a **sign/symbol of salvation** [`σύμβολον ἔχοντες σωτηρίας`] to remind them of the commandment of your law."
     - **v. 7:** "`ὁ γὰρ ἐπιστραφεὶς οὐ διὰ τὸ θεωρούμενον ἐσῴζετο, ἀλλὰ διὰ σὲ τὸν πάντων σωτῆρα`" ("For the one who turned toward it was saved, **not by what was beheld** [`διὰ τὸ θεωρούμενον`], **but by you, the Savior of all**").
     - **v. 12:** "For neither herb nor poultice healed them, but **your word** [`ὁ σὸς λόγος`], O Lord, which heals all things."
   - *Analytical Significance:* Wisdom 16 directly resolves the tension between Num 21 and 2 Kgs 18:4. By denying intrinsic power to "the thing beheld" (`τὸ θεωρούμενον`) and attributing deliverance to God the Savior (`σωτήρ`) and "your word" (`ὁ σὸς λόγος`), Wisdom preserves Num 21 as a valid *sign* (`σύμβολον`, paralleling LXX `σημεῖον`) while guarding against *Nehushtan*-style idolatry. Remarkably, it links this wilderness sign to the healing `λόγος`—a category central to the Fourth Gospel (John 1:1, 14).
2. **Philo of Alexandria (*Legum Allegoriae* 2.79–81; *De Agricultura* 95–99):**
   - Philo allegorizes the wilderness serpents as passion/pleasure (`ἡδονή`, echoing Eve's serpent) and Moses' bronze serpent as self-mastery/endurance (`σωφροσύνη` / `καρτερία`), hard and durable like bronze (`χαλκοῦς`). Turning one's gaze to endurance/reason neutralizes the deadly bite of pleasure. While Philo's Platonizing allegory differs sharply from John's historical-eschatological typology, it demonstrates that Second Temple Judaism actively read the bronze serpent as a symbolic antidote to the lethal "serpent" of sin/passion.

### 4.3 Early Rabbinic and Targumic Witnesses (Chronological Caveat)

- **Mishnah *Rosh Hashanah* 3:8** (codified c. 200 CE, thus post-dating John, though reflecting broader Jewish exegetical concern): Directly asks, "Could a serpent kill or make alive? Rather, when Israel looked upward (`mistakkəlîm kəlappê maʿlāh`) and subjected their hearts to their Father in heaven, they were healed; and if not, they wasted away."
- **Targumic Traditions (e.g., Targum Neofiti and Pseudo-Jonathan on Num 21:8–9):** Similarly paraphrase looking at the bronze serpent as lifting one's eyes/heart in prayer toward the Father in heaven / the Memra ("Word") of the Lord for healing.
- *Methodological Note:* Because these texts were codified after the first century CE, they cannot be cited as direct literary sources for John, but they corroborate the same hermeneutical trajectory attested earlier in Wisdom 16:7: neutralizing any magical reading of the bronze artifact by redirecting the "gaze" heavenward.

### 4.4 New Testament and Intra-Johannine Bridges

- **1 Corinthians 10:9:** Paul cites the Numbers 21 serpent plague as a warning against testing Christ/the Lord ("and were destroyed by the serpents [`ὑπὸ τῶν ὀφέων`]"), showing early Christian typological awareness of Num 21:4–9.
- **John 6:40:** Jesus declares that the Father's will is "that everyone who **beholds** the Son and **believes** in him may have eternal life" (`πᾶς ὁ θεωρῶν τὸν υἱὸν καὶ πιστεύων εἰς αὐτὸν ἔχῃ ζωὴν αἰώνιον`). Using `θεωρῶν` (the very verb Wisdom 16:7 uses for "what was beheld"!) alongside `πιστεύων` and `ζωὴν αἰώνιον`, John 6:40 explicitly bridges physical/attentive looking in Num 21 and believing in John 3:15.
- **John 8:28; 12:32–34; 19:37:** Together with 3:14, these form the three/four Johannine "lifting-up" (`ὑψόω`) sayings culminating at the cross, where the narrator explicitly signals that "lifted up from the earth" signifies the manner of Jesus' death (12:33) and draws "all people" (`πάντας`) to himself (12:32), while 19:37 invokes Zech 12:10's salvific/revelatory gaze (`ὄψονται`).

## 5. John 3:14–15: Typological Transformation and Contrasting Motifs

### 5.1 Attested Textual Evidence and Syntax

In his nocturnal dialogue with Nicodemus, Jesus moves from earthly/heavenly things (3:12) and the ascent/descent of the Son of Man (3:13) to the typological necessity of his elevation:

> **v. 14:** `καὶ καθὼς Μωϋσῆς ὕψωσεν τὸν ὄφιν ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ, οὕτως ὑψωθῆναι δεῖ τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ ἀνθρώπου,`
> ("And just as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up,")
>
> **v. 15:** `ἵνα πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων ἐν αὐτῷ ἔχῃ ζωὴν αἰώνιον.`
> ("that everyone who believes in him may have eternal life.")

### 5.2 Linguistic, Grammatical, and Lexical Transformations

1. **The Typological Frame (`καθώς ... οὕτως`):** Establishes an explicit analogical correspondence between the wilderness event and the Son of Man's passion-exaltation.
2. **From `θὲς ἐπὶ σημείου` (LXX Num 21:8) to `ὑψόω` (John 3:14):**
   - LXX Numbers 21 uses `τίθημι` / `ἵστημι` ("place/set on a standard"), *not* `ὑψόω`. John replaces the Septuagintal verb with **`ὑψόω`** ("lift up / exalt"), thereby uniting (a) the spatial lifting of the serpent onto a pole, (b) the physical lifting of Jesus onto a Roman cross (12:32–33), and (c) the Isaianic Servant's royal exaltation (`ὑψωθήσεται`, LXX Isa 52:13).
3. **Active Human Craft vs. Passive Divine Necessity (`ὕψωσεν` -> `ὑψωθῆναι δεῖ`):**
   - Moses actively lifted up (`ὕψωσεν`, aorist active) an artifact; the Son of Man *must be lifted up* (`ὑψωθῆναι δεῖ`, aorist passive infinitive governed by `δεῖ`, the marker of divine redemptive necessity; cf. John 3:7, 30; 20:9). The passive embraces both human agency ("when you have lifted up the Son of Man," 8:28) and the Father's saving purpose (3:16).
4. **Retention of `τὸν ὄφιν` ("the serpent"):**
   - John omits "fiery" (`śārāp`) and "bronze" (`nəḥōšet`), leaving the stark noun `τὸν ὄφιν` ("the serpent") in direct parallel with `τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ ἀνθρώπου` ("the Son of Man").
5. **From Physical Gaze (`rāʾāh` / `hibbîṭ`) to Ongoing Faith (`πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων`):**
   - `kol-hannāšûk wərāʾāh` ("everyone bitten who sees," Num 21:8) becomes `πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων` ("everyone who believes," present active participle indicating sustained, trusting allegiance).
   - *Syntactic/Textual Note on `ἐν αὐτῷ` (v. 15):* Whereas John typically uses `πιστεύω εἰς` (as in vv. 16, 18), critical texts (e.g., NA28) read `ἐν αὐτῷ` in v. 15 (with variant readings `εἰς αὐτόν` / `ἐπ' αὐτῷ` reflecting scribal assimilation to v. 16). Grammatically, `ἐν αὐτῷ` may modify `πιστεύων` ("believing in him") or `ἔχῃ` ("may have *in him* eternal life")—both yields emphasize union/location in the lifted-up Son of Man as the sphere of life.
6. **From Temporal Survival (`wāḥāy`) to `ζωὴν αἰώνιον` (Present Subjunctive `ἔχῃ`):**
   - Healing in Num 21 restored biological life (`wāḥāy`), delaying death from venom without conferring immortality. John 3:15 promises `ἔχῃ ζωὴν αἰώνιον` ("may have eternal life")—a present, enduring participation in the life of the age to come, explicitly contrasted in v. 16 with perishing (`μὴ ἀπόληται`).

### 5.3 Comparative Structural Matrix Across All Three Primary Texts

| Dimension | Numbers 21:4–9 | 2 Kings 18:4 | John 3:14–15 (with vv. 16–17) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Focal Object / Person** | `śārāp` / `nəḥaš nəḥōšet` (bronze serpent) on a `nēs` (`σημεῖον`). | `nəḥaš hannəḥōšet` called *Nəḥuštān*. | `τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ ἀνθρώπου` (the Son of Man) lifted up (`ὑψωθῆναι`). |
| **Location / Scope** | Wilderness (`bammidbār` / `ἐν τῇ ἐρήμῳ`); Israelite camp. | Jerusalem / Judah; Israelite cultus. | Cross/world (`τὸν κόσμον`, v. 16–17); universal (`πᾶς`). |
| **Human Condition** | Bitten by fiery serpents (`hannāšûk`); physical mortality. | Idolatrous veneration (`məqaṭṭərîm lô`); covenantal infidelity. | Mortal/perishing under sin/judgment (`μὴ ἀπόληται`, vv. 16–18). |
| **Required Human Act** | Looking/gazing (`rāʾāh` / `hibbîṭ`) toward the standard. | Crushing (`kittat`) the artifact to restore exclusive trust in YHWH. | Believing (`πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων`) in the lifted-up Son of Man. |
| **Outcome** | Physical preservation (`wāḥāy`). | Cultic purification; covenant fidelity (vv. 5–6). | Possession of eternal life (`ἔχῃ ζωὴν αἰώνιον`). |

### 5.4 Interpretive Hypotheses on the Paradox of the Serpent-Son of Man Analogy

Why does John 3:14 compare the Son of Man—whom the Gospel portrays as the sinless Word and giver of life—to *a serpent*, an animal overwhelmingly associated with uncleanness, judgment, and in Genesis 3 / Revelation 12:9 with humanity's primal adversary? Three complementary interpretive hypotheses address this paradox:

1. **Structural and Functional Analogy (Not Ontological Equivalence):** The `καθώς ... οὕτως` comparison correlates *elevation for deliverance*, not the nature of a serpent with the nature of Jesus. Just as Moses raised an outward sign (`σημεῖον` / `nēs`) so the dying could look and live, the Son of Man is raised on the cross so the perishing may believe and receive eternal life.
2. **Curse-Bearing / Likeness of the Plague (Pauline-Johannine Resonance):** Just as the bronze serpent bore the *likeness* of the deadly creature without its venom, so on the cross the Son of Man enters into the full solidarity of human mortality and the curse of sin's judgment without sin (cf. Rom 8:3, "in the likeness of sinful flesh"; 2 Cor 5:21; Gal 3:13). Looking upon the crucified one means confronting the visible exposure of the world's sin and judgment (cf. John 12:31–32).
3. **Edenic Reversal and Defeat of the Serpent (Intertextual Hypothesis):** In Genesis 3, the serpent (`nāḥāš` / `ὄφις`) brings death through distrust of God's word; in John 8:44, the devil is "a murderer from the beginning" and father of lies; in John 12:31–32, the "lifting up" of Jesus casts out "the ruler of this world." While John 3:14 does not explicitly cite Genesis 3, reading John 3:14 within the wider Johannine narrative suggests an ironic reversal: the lifted-up Son of Man undoes the mortal bite of the primal serpent by restoring trust (`πιστεύων`) in God's word.

### 5.5 Contrasting Motifs: Why *Nehushtan* Had to Be Destroyed While the Son of Man Must Be Lifted Up

Read together, 2 Kings 18:4 provides an essential theological foil to John 3:14–15:

- **Idolatry of the Artifact vs. Divinity of the Son:** In 2 Kings 18:4, the bronze serpent became an idolatrous substitute because a created metal sign received the worship (`məqaṭṭərîm lô`) due exclusively to YHWH. In John's Gospel, by contrast, the Son of Man who is lifted up is the incarnate `Λόγος` (John 1:1, 14) who shares the Father's glory and rightly receives honor equal to the Father (John 5:23; 20:28). Directing faith to him (`πιστεύων ἐν αὐτῷ`) does not compete with YHWH, because "whoever believes in me, believes not in me but in him who sent me" (John 12:44; cf. 14:9).
- **From Relic to Person:** Hezekiah destroys an inanimate object (`nəḥuštān`, "mere bronze") that had outlived its moment; John shifts the locus of salvation away from any material relic or physical temple (cf. John 2:19–21; 4:21–24) to the living, crucified, and exalted Person of the Son of Man.

## 6. Methodological Summary and Open Scholarly Questions

- **Textually Grounded Core:** The lexical chain linking `śārāp` / `nəḥaš nəḥōšet` / `nēs` / `hibbîṭ` (Num 21:8–9), `kittat` / `məqaṭṭərîm lô` / `nəḥuštān` (2 Kgs 18:4), `σύμβολον σωτηρίας` / `οὐ διὰ τὸ θεωρούμενον` / `λόγος` (Wis 16:6–12), `ὑψωθήσεται` (LXX Isa 52:13), `wəhibbîṭû` / `ὄψονται` (Zech 12:10 / John 19:37), and `ὑψωθῆναι δεῖ` / `πιστεύων` / `ζωὴν αἰώνιον` (John 3:14–15; cf. `θεωρῶν` in John 6:40) is firmly attested across the primary Hebrew and Greek witnesses.
- **Open Interpretive Questions:**
  1. Whether `wayyiqrāʾ-lô nəḥuštān` in 2 Kgs 18:4 reflects Hezekiah's own polemical renaming or a pre-existing Jerusalemite cultic title cannot be decided from Hebrew syntax alone.
  2. Whether the Fourth Evangelist knew Wisdom of Solomon 16 directly or drew independently on a wider Hellenistic Jewish exegetical tradition (also reflected in Philo and later rabbinic midrash) remains an open historical question, though Wisdom 16:5–12 supplies the closest conceptual and lexical bridge.
  3. The exact nuance of `ἐν αὐτῷ` in John 3:15 (modifying `πιστεύων` vs. `ἔχῃ`) remains syntactically open and may reflect deliberate Johannine compression.
